What decides these cases

Families Who Act in the First Week Win ICE Detention Cases

Find an ICE Detainee Before They're Transferred

Texas holds more people in ICE custody than any other state, and detainees are moved constantly — between Texas facilities and into Texas from across the country, often within days and without notice. Every transfer can mean a new court, a longer drive, and lost time. Locating your loved one and getting a lawyer on record (Form G-28) before the next transfer is the single most time-sensitive move a family can make.

Immigration Bond Is Won With Evidence, Not a Plea

The judge weighs family ties, a stable address, work history, community roots, any record, and whether a sponsor will take responsibility. Families who show up with sponsor letters, proof of address, and documents win bonds; families who show up with only love and worry usually don't. We build that evidence package with you in days, so it's ready the moment the court is.

Don't Let Them Sign Anything in ICE Detention

Detained people are handed papers — stipulated removal orders, voluntary departure agreements — that can waive the right to ever see a judge. Signing to "get out faster" can mean leaving faster, permanently. The message to get to your loved one today, in any facility: sign nothing, and say you want to speak with a lawyer.

Common questions

ICE Detention & Immigration Bond Questions We Answer Every Week

How do I find someone in ICE detention in Texas?

Use ICE's Online Detainee Locator (locator.ice.gov): search by A-number and country of birth, or by name, date of birth, and country. The A-number — 9 digits on any immigration paperwork — is the key, so look for old documents. Two Texas realities: people booked in the last day or two may not appear yet, and detainees are transferred between facilities — and into Texas from other states — without notice, so a location can go stale overnight. If you can't find them, that's exactly the first thing we do.

Can my loved one get out on bond?

Many detainees can — an immigration judge weighs family ties, a stable address, work history, community roots, and any record, and a strong sponsor package moves every one of those factors. But certain criminal history triggers mandatory detention with no bond, and people stopped at the border face a different process. We analyze which category applies in the first review and tell you straight.

How much is immigration bond, and how is it paid?

The legal minimum is $1,500; judges commonly set bonds in the several-thousand range or higher depending on the case. It's paid in full at an ICE bond office — and here's the detail families must plan for: the person who pays must show they're lawfully present in the U.S. If you don't have status yourself, line up a relative or friend who does. The money is refunded at the end of the case as long as your loved one attends every hearing.

How fast can a bond hearing happen in Texas?

A bond request can be made at the first hearing or by written motion, and courts often schedule bond hearings within days to a few weeks — the wait varies by court, and Texas's detained dockets move fast. What the family controls is readiness: the sponsor, the address proof, and the letters. When the package is ready the moment the court is, no time is wasted. That's why we start building it on day one.

Should they sign anything ICE gives them?

Not before a lawyer reads it. Stipulated removal orders and voluntary departure papers can waive the right to see a judge at all — people sign thinking it speeds up release and instead it speeds up removal. Everyone in detention has the right to say two things: "I do not want to sign" and "I want to speak with a lawyer." Get that message to your loved one through the facility phone as soon as you can.

We don't live in Texas — our loved one was transferred there. Can you still help?

Yes. Immigration law is federal, so we can represent detainees held in Texas facilities and appear in the courts that serve them regardless of where the family lives — many detained hearings are also conducted by video. Families work with us by phone, in English or Spanish, from anywhere. What we need from you is what you know: name, A-number if you have it, and where they were picked up.
