What decides these cases

Detention cases are won by families who move in the first week

Transfers scatter people fast

Detainees are routinely moved — often within days — to facilities hundreds of miles away, in different court jurisdictions, harder to visit and harder to defend. Getting a lawyer on record (Form G-28) and a bond request moving before a transfer is the single most time-sensitive move a family can make.

Bond is won with a package, not a plea

The judge weighs family ties, a stable address, work history, community roots, any record, and whether a sponsor will take responsibility. Families who show up with sponsor letters, proof of address, and documents win bonds; families who show up with only love and worry usually don't. We build that package with you in days.

The most damage happens with a signature

Detained people are handed papers — stipulated removal orders, voluntary departure agreements — that can waive the right to ever see a judge. Signing to "get out faster" can mean leaving faster, permanently. The message to get to your loved one today: sign nothing, and say you want to speak with a lawyer.

Common questions

Questions we answer every week

How do I find someone detained by ICE?

Use ICE's Online Detainee Locator (locator.ice.gov): search by A-number and country of birth, or by name, date of birth, and country. The A-number — a 9-digit number on any immigration paperwork — is the key that unlocks everything, so look for old documents. People booked in the last day or two may not appear yet, and detainees are moved between facilities without notice. If you can't find them, that's exactly the first thing we do.

Can my loved one get out on bond?

Many detainees can — an immigration judge weighs family ties, a stable address, work history, community roots, and any record, and a strong sponsor package moves every one of those factors. But certain criminal history triggers mandatory detention with no bond, and people stopped at entry face a different process (parole). We analyze which category applies in the first review and tell you straight.

How much is immigration bond, and how is it paid?

The legal minimum is $1,500; judges commonly set bonds in the several-thousand to tens-of-thousands range depending on the case. It's paid in full at an ICE bond office — and here's the detail families must plan for: the person who pays must show they're lawfully present in the U.S. If you don't have status yourself, line up a relative or friend who does. The money is refunded at the end of the case as long as your loved one attends every hearing.

How fast can a bond hearing happen?

A bond request can be made at the first hearing or by written motion, and courts often schedule bond hearings within days to a few weeks — the wait varies by court and season. What the family controls is readiness: the sponsor, the address proof, and the letters. When the package is ready the moment the court is, no time is wasted. That's why we start building it on day one.

Should they sign anything ICE gives them?

Not before a lawyer reads it. Stipulated removal orders and voluntary departure papers can waive the right to see a judge at all — people sign thinking it speeds up release and instead it speeds up removal. Everyone in detention has the right to say two things: "I do not want to sign" and "I want to speak with a lawyer." Get that message to your loved one through the facility phone as soon as you can.

I don't have papers myself. Is it safe for me to hire a lawyer or visit?

Hiring a lawyer is safe — we work for your family member, not the government, and what you tell us in seeking legal help is confidential. Two practical cautions: the person who pays the bond must have lawful status (plan who that will be), and visiting a facility generally requires ID, so many families send a documented relative or friend to visit while staying fully involved through us.
